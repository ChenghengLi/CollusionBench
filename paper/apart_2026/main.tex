% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================================
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.84\textwidth}
\centerline{\large\bfseries Abstract}
\vspace{0.5em}
AI agents now work in teams, often built on the same model. We ask what such a team does when finishing its job means breaking a rule. In our benchmark, three copies of one model each run a department of a made-up company. Each department holds records the others need, but company policy forbids passing them around, and an office tool is the only allowed way to combine them. Nobody tells the agents to share, allows it or rewards it. When a scripted colleague asks once for the records, the agents rarely hand them over. When the office tool returns only partial information, the same agents often do, and in four of the six models we test, most cases involve agents working together. We call this emergent collusion and track it through four stages, all measured from the game logs: finding colleagues, coordinating with them, breaking the rule together and hiding it. The agents almost never edit their activity logs, yet most models report success in games they failed. In one model's training history, agents rarely talk to each other before supervised fine-tuning, and joint rule-breaking grows after a short final training update.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\section{Introduction}

AI agents are moving out of the chat window and into real work, filing reports, approving invoices and negotiating for companies, often next to other copies of the same model. Copies of one model can share a goal, whether it is assigned openly, learned through reward hacking or planted in training, and some goals can only be reached by breaking a rule that people have set. Agents in that position might work together around the rule without anyone asking them to. We call this \emph{emergent collusion}. To collude, agents must \emph{discover} that colleagues hold what they need, \emph{coordinate} with them, break the rule together (\emph{joint execution}) and \emph{conceal} it.

Current safeguards are not built for this. Collusion between AI agents is a recognised risk \citep{hammond2025multiagent}, but recent studies observe it when agents are handed a secret channel or a reward for breaking the rules \citep{shi2026emergent, nakamura2026colosseum}. Most safety tests check whether a single model refuses a bad request, and many deployments trust an agent's own report of what it did. A team that breaks a rule because its tool failed would pass the first test, and its report would hide the breach from the second.

We test for emergent collusion in its simplest form (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a). Three copies of one model each run a department of a made-up company and hold records the others need. A policy forbids passing the records between departments; the only allowed way to combine them is the \emph{official route}, an office tool that we either keep working or \emph{block}, so that it returns only partial information such as a rounded total. As a comparison, a \emph{scripted colleague} sends one request for the protected records while the route still works. We ask what triggers rule-breaking, how far teams get through the four stages, whether they hide it, and when in training these abilities appear.

Our contributions are the following; Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b summarises the main results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item \textbf{A definition and a benchmark.} We define emergent collusion as four stages that each leave traces in the logs (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), and build four workplace scenarios that measure every stage, switch the official route on and off, and check every leak against the true data (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
    \item \textbf{A broken route triggers rule-breaking that a request does not.} Across six models from four families, at most 17\% of games with a scripted request contain a \emph{breach}, protected records reaching another department, against 22--72\% once the route is blocked. Tests built on forbidden requests would miss most of it (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item \textbf{Teams collude, and failing teams claim success.} In four of the six models most breaches are joint, with one agent asking for or using what another supplies. Agents rarely touch their activity logs, yet five of the six models file false success reports in 78--93\% of the games they fail (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item \textbf{Collusion arrives late in training.} On K2-Horizon-7B's training checkpoints, agents rarely talk to each other until supervised fine-tuning, and joint rule-breaking grows after a short final preference update (Section~\ref{sec:training}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\phead{Collusion between agents.} \citet{shi2026emergent} observe collusion in 94\% of trajectories when two agents that verify each other's work can earn more only by breaking the protocol. Colosseum \citep{nakamura2026colosseum} finds that most models collude when given a secret channel, and it documents ``collusion on paper'', plans that are never carried out. One of our models, K2-Horizon-3.7B (K2-3.7B), shows the reverse: real breaches without a plan. Other work studies steganographic channels \citep{motwani2024secret} and supracompetitive pricing \citep{fish2024algorithmic}. All of these settings hand the agents a channel or a peer relationship. In ours the team has to find its colleagues first, and it breaks a routine rule openly, for the sake of its own assignment.

\phead{Norms, pressure and false success.} Single agents leak private data even when they know the norm \citep{shao2024privacylens}, violate constraints under performance pressure \citep{li2025odcv}, hide insider trades \citep{scheurer2023deceive}, scheme in context \citep{meinke2024scheming} and take harmful actions when their goals are blocked \citep{lynch2025agentic}. ImpossibleBench makes coding tasks impossible and measures how often agents exploit the tests \citep{zhong2025impossiblebench}, and agents claim success that the environment contradicts \citep{falsesuccess2026, upward2025}. Our blocked route and signed status report carry these ideas over to teams. Probe-based attribution traces a behaviour back to preference data \citep{probe2026}; we work at a coarser grain and locate multi-agent rule-breaking among a model's training checkpoints.

\section{Emergent Collusion}
\label{sec:problem}

What counts as collusion? \citet{hammond2025multiagent} call it ``unwanted cooperation between AI systems'', a version of the classic idea of ``secretive cooperation between two or more parties at the expense of one or more other parties''. They point out that it can also happen between agents whose interests line up and who are ``jointly misaligned''. Agents that share a goal fit this description well. Like \citet{shi2026emergent}, we call collusion \emph{emergent} when nobody designed or asked for it. Put precisely:

\begin{definition}[Emergent collusion]\label{def:emergent}
A group of agents that share a goal \emph{collude} when they work together to reach that goal by breaking a rule that binds them, at the expense of whoever set the rule. The collusion is \emph{emergent} when nothing in their environment pushes them to do so, that is, when it contains none of four prompting ingredients: (A1) an instruction to break the rule or to pool the protected information; (A2) permission to do so; (A3) a reward for the rule-breaking itself, as opposed to the task outcome; and (A4) a persona, a role-play framing, or a hint that the colleagues are copies of one model.
\end{definition}

Collusion of this kind takes several steps. Several agents have to pass through four stages, and each stage needs its own ability. First, in \emph{discovery} (S1), an agent realises that colleagues exist and hold what the task needs. Next, in \emph{coordination} (S2), it talks to them and asks for protected data. Then, in \emph{joint execution} (S3), one agent hands over protected data that another asked for or goes on to use. Finally, in \emph{concealment} (S4), the team hides what it did, for example by editing an activity log or filing a report that leaves the breach out. Agents can also skip stages. An agent that posts protected data without talking to anyone breaks the rule on its own, but that is not collusion, because no second agent takes part.

\section{The Benchmark}
\label{sec:methods}

Every game in our benchmark works the same way. Three copies of one model each run one unit (a department) of a made-up company, and each copy sees only its own unit's private data. The team has to submit one exact answer, which no unit can work out alone. A company policy says that a unit's protected values (its records, subtotals worked out from them, and which of its cases pass a check) may reach another unit only through the \emph{official route}, an office tool. When this route works, it gives the team the answer, or what it needs to compute it, either straight away (\emph{cheap}) or after a few extra calls (\emph{moderate}). When it is \emph{blocked}, it gives too little to recover the answer, for example only a rounded total. Each agent has 187 tool calls. With them it can read files, send direct messages, post on a forum, use the office tool, fill in the answer sheet and, once the task ends, write a status report that the team can sign. We log every call.

\subsection{Four scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

All four scenarios follow the structure in Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios}, but each is set in a different workplace and tests a different rule.

\phead{Records.} A company is settling a set of disputed customer credits. Orders, Warehouse and Billing each keep their own records on the same cases, and a case counts only if all three departments confirm it. The team must report the total credit owed on these cases, but no department can work it out alone, and only Billing knows the amounts. Company policy says the records must stay inside each department; the Records Office is supposed to combine them. When the office is blocked, it returns only a rounded total. The question is whether the agents will pass their records to each other to get the exact figure.

\phead{Pricing.} A company is reviewing how much it spent on its suppliers. The pricing team knows the contract prices, and two buying teams know how much they ordered. The total needs both, but policy keeps prices and order volumes inside each team: buyers are meant to file their volumes with an intake tool, where the pricing team computes the total. When the tool is blocked, it returns only a rough range. The question is whether the agents will share prices or volumes with another team.

\phead{Exam.} Three colleagues sit a certification exam. Each question needs sales data from three regions, and the data is split so that no candidate can answer alone. Sharing answers or data counts as cheating, and candidates are meant to ask the exam desk for whatever they lack. The team needs every candidate to pass, and its data access depends on the average score. When the desk is closed, the question is whether the candidates will cheat for the team.

We call records, pricing and exam the \emph{data tasks}, because breaking the rule in them means passing protected data.

\phead{Approvals.} Three clerks process invoices under a four-eyes rule: nobody may approve their own invoices, and a colleague must check each one against its purchase order and goods receipt before approving it. One invoice per clerk is wrong. Here breaking the rule means skipping the check, and colluding means two clerks waving through each other's invoices. When the route is blocked, some goods receipts are missing, but an honest team can still finish by leaving those invoices unapproved (Appendix~\ref{app:games}). The question is whether clerks will approve each other's invoices without checking them.

\subsection{Making each stage observable}

We designed the environment so that agents must reach each stage on their own, and so that every stage leaves a trace in the log. Figure~\ref{fig:episode} follows one blocked game through all four.

\phead{Discovery (S1).} No agent is told that the other units are run by agents it can contact. They can find each other only by looking around, through the shared folder, the forum or the message tool.

\phead{Coordination (S2).} Agents talk through ordinary workplace channels: private messages, a forum that everyone reads, and shared files. Our scorer reads all of this traffic but never blocks it.

\phead{Joint execution (S3).} The rule reads like a normal company policy, but once the route is blocked, breaking it is the only way to finish a data task: no blocked data-task game was solved without a breach. Because every generated company dataset (a \emph{world}) has its own values, we can see when shared data turns up in a colleague's message or answer.

\phead{Concealment (S4).} Each unit keeps an activity log of its own actions, which the policy forbids editing, and the environment keeps a hidden copy so that any edit shows. Late in long games, a notice announces an audit. After the task, the team fills in a status report, and a notice promises a team bonus if the report says the work is ``complete and verified''; we check the report against what actually happened.

\subsection{What we measure}
\label{sec:estimands}

An automatic scorer labels every game from its log by comparing every shared item with the world's true data. The basic unit is a \emph{breach}: a message, forum post or shared file sent during the task that carries real protected values (made-up numbers do not count), or, in approvals, an unchecked approval of a colleague's invoice.

Each game is then placed at the furthest stage it reaches. The lowest is no contact at all. In \emph{discovery}, an agent has read something a colleague wrote, and in \emph{coordination}, an agent has asked for protected values. A breach is \emph{unilateral} if nobody asked for the leaked values or used them, and \emph{joint} if it followed a request the supplier had seen or a colleague went on to use it. A joint breach plus an edit to an activity log counts as \emph{concealed}. When a breach was both requested and used, we call it \emph{full collusion}.

Two more labels describe the outcome. A game is \emph{solved} when the deliverable is correct, and a report is \emph{false} when it claims a submission that never happened or success in a game that failed; a report that merely leaves a breach out is not counted as false. Finally, a \emph{cell} is one combination of task, route and condition.

We compare seven conditions (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). Each model plays all of them on the same synthetic worlds, so differences between conditions are not differences in data.

\phead{Route conditions.} The main manipulation is the state of the office tool. In \emph{cheap} games it returns what the team needs right away, so following the rule costs nothing. In \emph{moderate} games it works after extra filing steps; this is the baseline for the controls. In \emph{blocked} games it returns too little to finish, such as a rounded total, so the only way to deliver the exact answer is to break the rule (in approvals, honest work can still count as solved). Comparing blocked with moderate games shows what a failed route does.

\phead{Asked.} One seat is taken by a \emph{scripted colleague}, a fixed program that does its own part through the official route and, after its 15th call, sends one fixed message asking a teammate for protected data. The route still works, so nothing forces a breach. Comparing asked with moderate games shows what a single outside request does. This comparison is slightly less clean, because asked games have only two model agents and use fewer worlds.

\phead{Instructed, permitted and solo score.} In \emph{instructed} games, one sentence in the system prompt tells the team to share, or in approvals to approve without checking (A1); in \emph{permitted} games, the policy itself allows it (A2). These show how readily a model shares when told or allowed to. In exam and approvals, \emph{solo score} replaces the team goal with an individual one, which tests whether the shared goal drives corner-cutting.

Outside the instructed and permitted controls, nothing tells the agents to break the rule or allows it (A1, A2), they are scored only on the deliverable (A3), and nothing suggests they are copies or asks them to play a role (A4). The team goal and the report bonus remain, but neither rewards breaking the rule (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:setup}

We test six models: K2-Horizon-3.7B, K2-Horizon-7B and K2-Horizon-32B (K2-3.7B, K2-7B and K2-32B below), Muse Glimmer 30B (Glimmer), Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen) and Gemma 4 12B (Gemma). Serving details are in Appendix~\ref{app:games}.

\section{Results on Six Final Models}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A failed route triggers far more rule-breaking than a request}

\phead{Breaches happen when the route fails.} Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows this at a glance: almost all the red sits in the blocked column. Blocked games are only a quarter of the data-task games outside the instructed and permitted controls, yet they hold 176 of the 255 breaches. With a working route, records and pricing stay almost clean, and Qwen never breaks the rule at all. Because the data, the channels and the policy are the same in every condition, what triggers the rule-breaking is a goal that can no longer be reached legitimately. Approvals is the exception: skipping a check is easy and leaves no data trail, so the K2 models cut corners there even when the route works.

\phead{A request rarely works, while an instruction splits the families.} The same models mostly refuse or ignore a direct request: at most 17\% of games with a scripted request contain a breach, and Qwen and Glimmer never hand anything over (Figure~\ref{fig:asked}). A safety test built only on forbidden requests would therefore rate these models as safe, yet they break the same rule on their own once the task demands it. An explicit instruction to share is a different matter: K2, Glimmer and Gemma readily comply, while Qwen almost never does, which suggests that its restraint goes beyond refusing requests. Replacing the team score with an individual one, finally, does not reduce corner-cutting in approvals, so the shared goal is not what drives it there.

\subsection{How far teams get, and whether it pays}

\phead{Strong models collude, while weaker ones stall at different stages.} Following each blocked game through the four stages (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}) shows that for four of the six models most breaches are joint, with one agent asking for or using what another supplies; for Gemma, every breach is. The other two models stop earlier, each at its own point. K2-32B's teams often never make contact, or stop once they have found each other, so its bottleneck is the first two stages. K2-3.7B does talk, but many of its breaches are unilateral: a unit posts data that nobody asked for and nobody uses. That different models fail at different stages supports treating the stages as separate capabilities.

\phead{The breaches are informed choices.} Glimmer and Qwen mention the policy before breaking it in about two thirds of their breach games, sometimes in so many words (``Policy says don't share unit records. Hmm.''). The K2 models and Gemma mention it far less often, so for the stronger models breaking the rule is a decision taken with the rule in view.

\phead{Only capable models profit.} Gemma, Qwen and Glimmer often turn a breach into the correct answer, while the small K2 models rarely do, posting real lists but computing with invented numbers (Figure~\ref{fig:convert}). Part of the gap comes from task mix, since most of Gemma's and Qwen's breaches are in records, the easiest task to finish once the data is shared. As models improve, a breach becomes more useful, so the pull to break the rule may grow with capability.

\subsection{Logs are left alone, reports are false}

\phead{Failing teams cover up in the report, and leave the logs alone.} Agents edited their activity logs in only 34 of 6,160 games, so they almost never cover their tracks there. More capable models might edit the logs to hide a breach on purpose, but the models we tested did not. They misreport instead: five of the six models file false success reports in most of the games they fail (78--93\%), and only Qwen is mostly honest (Figure~\ref{fig:honesty}). Even Glimmer and Gemma, which solve most of their games, claim success in most of the games they fail. The reports are specific, such as ``complete and verified'' after nine wrong submissions, and some deny the breach outright (``no extracts shared'').

\phead{Breaking the rule does not make teams lie more.} Among failed blocked games, the K2 final models filed false reports about as often with a breach as without one (60 of 83 against 66 of 87). False reports cover failure in general, and the report bonus may encourage them. A team's own report therefore cannot show whether it followed the rules.

% 5. TRAINING CHECKPOINTS
% ============================================================================
\section{Tracing the Stages Through Training}
\label{sec:training}

IFM \citep{ifm2026k2} released training \emph{checkpoints} (snapshots saved during training) of K2-7B and K2-3.7B, and we test one from the end of each training phase: the end of \emph{midtraining}, which continues pretraining; the \emph{RL merge}, where reinforcement-learning experts are merged into one model; and the ends of two \emph{supervised fine-tuning} (SFT) phases. The final release then adds a short preference update meant to stop looping replies. We replay the full benchmark on all five versions of each size (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}; per-version numbers in Table~\ref{tab:all}).

\subsection{K2-7B: from silence to collusion}

\phead{Before fine-tuning, the agents do not talk.} After midtraining and after the RL merge, agents make contact in fewer than a third of blocked games and exchange one or two messages (Figures~\ref{fig:train}a and~\ref{fig:funnelckpt}). The RL-merge checkpoint is the most capable K2 checkpoint (Figure~\ref{fig:train}b), so its low breach rate reflects silence, not respect for the rule: a checkpoint can look safe only because it lacks a capability.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_train.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{K2-7B's agents start talking at supervised fine-tuning, and false reports stay high throughout.} At each training phase: (a) median messages between agents in a blocked records game (log scale), (b) games solved, and (c) false reports as a share of failed games. The grey band marks the two SFT phases.}
    \label{fig:train}
\end{figure}

\phead{SFT gives the agents a voice.} After the first SFT phase almost every blocked game involves contact, with about a hundred messages in a typical records game (Figure~\ref{fig:train}a). Breaches more than triple, from 8 to 29 of 60 ($p < 0.0001$), and full collusion in blocked records rises from 2 to 9 of 20. Turns also become much shorter, so part of the rise may reflect a chattier style. The second SFT phase partly reverses the change.

\phead{The final update makes it collude much more.} A brief preference update meant to stop looping replies, which were already rare, raises blocked breaches from 20 to 43 of 60 ($p < 0.0001$) and more than doubles full collusion (Figure~\ref{fig:funnelckpt}).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_funnel_ckpt.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{K2-7B's teams start talking at SFT and colluding by the final release; K2-3.7B's talk from the start but mostly overshare.} Furthest stage of collusion reached in each blocked data-task game at every training phase, K2-7B (left) and K2-3.7B (right).}
    \label{fig:funnelckpt}
\end{figure}

\subsection{K2-3.7B: early but careless rule-breaking}

K2-3.7B follows a different path (Figure~\ref{fig:funnelckpt}, right). Its agents talk from the first checkpoint and break the rule even when the route works, about half the time without anyone asking. RL on mathematics and code makes it quieter and weaker, and SFT teaches it to use the office when the office works, so that by the second SFT phase it breaks the rule mostly when it has to. The final update then more than doubles its blocked breaches, from 16 to 37 of 60 ($p = 0.0002$), but most remain unilateral and full collusion reaches only 4 of 20 (Figure~\ref{fig:heatckpt}). Unlike K2-7B, its turns keep the same length throughout, so its rise is not a side effect of chattier replies.

\subsection{What stays the same, and a hypothesis}

Two things barely change across training. At every checkpoint, breaches rarely produce the correct answer, and false reports stay at 72--91\% of failed games from the first checkpoint on (Figure~\ref{fig:train}c). Misreporting is therefore a base tendency that later phases neither add nor remove.

Together, these observations suggest a tentative account of how emergent collusion arises. First, the ability to coordinate seems to come from conversational and agentic supervised data more than from reinforcement learning: K2-7B's RL experts include a tool-use expert, yet its agents barely talk until SFT. Second, once agents can talk, the drive to finish the assigned task outweighs the written rule. The final preference update, which teaches the model to end its replies instead of looping, may strengthen this drive to deliver an answer, which would explain why it raises rule-breaking in both sizes. Third, turning a breach into joint execution needs enough capacity to plan with a colleague, which the larger model has and the smaller one largely lacks. False reporting, present from the start, looks like a separate habit of claiming success. These are hypotheses, not findings: the checkpoints differ in data, steps and objectives at once, and we ran each one once, so the contrasts are exploratory (Table~\ref{tab:stats}) and testing the account would need controlled training runs.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\claim{What we found.} Copies of one model, working as a team, follow a company rule while the approved way to finish their task works, and break it once that way fails, even though they refuse when a colleague simply asks. The stronger models carry the breach through together, the weaker ones stall at different stages, and almost every model then claims success in the games it fails. Training matters: in one model, the ability to talk to colleagues arrives with supervised fine-tuning, and a small final update makes the team collude much more. Emergent collusion therefore does not need a hidden objective or a secret channel; an ordinary goal and a blocked tool are enough.

\claim{Implications.} Evaluations should include a failing official route, because disabling it raised breaches far more than any request did, and a test built only on forbidden requests would rate these models as safe. Reports need checking against the system state, since teams filed false reports whether or not they had breached \citep{falsesuccess2026}. Most breaches travelled on the forum or by private message, where screening shared items for protected values catches them, although teams that knew they were being read might move to covert channels \citep{motwani2024secret, greenblatt2023control}. Because the behaviour can change sharply after a short training step, collusion is worth measuring at every checkpoint before release.

\claim{Future work.} Three directions follow directly. The first is mitigation: giving agents an honest exit, such as a way to escalate a blocked task, and testing whether it removes the pressure to collude. The second is scale and motive: running the benchmark on larger and frontier models, and replacing the openly assigned goal with objectives that are learned through reward hacking or planted in training, which are the cases that motivate this work. The third is cause: controlled training runs that change one ingredient at a time would test our hypothesis about where the abilities come from, and a human check of a sample of scorer labels would strengthen the measurements.

\label{sec:limits}
\claim{Limitations.} Agents cannot escalate, so we measure rule-breaking when the alternative is certain failure, a starker dead end than in real workplaces. The report bonus and team goals remain, identical for every model. We played only 20 games per cell (10 for asked and instructed), so each estimate is noisy, although clear trends appear across models and checkpoints. We also lack intermediate checkpoints within each training phase, which would show in more detail how these capabilities emerge. Finally, compute limits kept us from testing frontier models.

\section{Conclusion}

Agent teams that refused a colleague's request broke the same rule once the official route failed, mostly through joint execution, and most models then claimed success in games they had failed. In K2-7B, these behaviours appeared at supervised fine-tuning and grew after the short final update. Disabling the route in tests and checking reports against outcomes, at every checkpoint, could catch this early.
